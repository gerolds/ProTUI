# Narrative voice: player-facing text

Applies to everything a player reads as fiction: intros, work orders, reports, dialogue, item and people descriptions, labels that carry the world. `AGENTS.md` "Writing" covers engineering text and does not apply here; its terse style is the main way fiction goes wrong. This guide applies to any game and borrows craft principles from three authors, never their recognizable prose.

## The rule: material, not conclusions

Give the player the material from which they reach a conclusion. Do not state the conclusion. (Source: the designer, after a rejected intro.)

- Concreteness before abstraction. A named person, an object, a number on a docket, a sound, before any summary word such as "neglected", "dangerous" or "struggling".
- Relationships and causality. Who owes whom, who asked, what broke because of what. A fact earns its place when it connects to another.
- A human point of reference. Someone the player can picture: their job, their habit, what they want.
- Discovery rather than declaration. Let the player notice; do not explain the world's themes.
- Room for curiosity. Leave a question open that the player wants answered by playing.

The failure looks like this (the shape, not the content): "Old station. Debts. Nobody comes. You keep it running." Every sentence is a conclusion. Rewrite it as a moment with a person, an object and a cause.

## John Scalzi: people under pressure, plainly told

- Common words, contractions and human friction. The reader needs no genre vocabulary to follow ([Lightspeed interview][lightspeed]).
- Dialogue does a present job: it asks, refuses, jokes to cope, or bargains. Cut speeches that only pretend to be replies ([Writing Excuses 5.38][dialogue]).
- Explain a thing when it matters to the next choice. Leave history that changes nothing ([Friction interview][friction]).
- Give obstructive or cruel people reasons the player can follow ([Friction interview][friction]).
- Humour comes from competent people in bad situations. It never carries a warning alone: state risks and costs plainly beside it.

## Brandon Sanderson: rules the player can use

- A cause can solve a problem only once the player understands it. Teach how a thing works before the story relies on it ([First Law][laws]).
- Limits are more interesting than powers. Show what a tool, a person or the company cannot do before asking the player to bet on it ([Laws][laws]).
- Extend what already exists before adding something new. Let a known object or person gain a second use ([Third Law][third]).
- Promise, progress, payoff. Each thread states what it is about, shows steps, and pays off in a form the player can see ([plot lecture][plot]).
- Every speaker wants something, and the want changes what they say ([character lecture][character]).

## Adrian Tchaikovsky: the world as a process

- A world is not a backdrop. It is a process caught at a crisis point. Show what the world is brewing now, and the plot comes out of that ([Knych roundtable][knych]).
- Choose the characters who show different sides of the setting: who works it, who profits, who pays ([Knych roundtable][knych]).
- Render machines, systems and non-human minds from the inside, by their own logic (his zoology shows in most of his work). A failing relay, a corporate procedure or an animal behaves according to what it is, not according to what the plot needs.
- Show technology through practical problems: what jams, what it costs, who fixes it and how.
- Do not take the reader on a tour of the world. He names his own weakness as "showing my working", which leaves the characters waiting, and he prunes it. Cut world detail that does not move the current moment ([Clarkesworld interview][clarkesworld]).

## Lines on screen

- Live lines run 6–16 words. A paused screen, such as an intro or a report, may run longer.
- Each line makes sense with its speaker label on the current screen. Name the person or thing a pronoun would hide.
- Keep keys, quantities, prices, effects and fatal risks plain and beside the fiction, never inside it.
- Lines carry the fiction. Labels such as JOB, CLIENT, HAZARD and NEXT are interface, not fiction. Keep them few.

## Status of drafts

Player-facing prose written by an agent is a draft for the designer, and the content file's header says so. Local detail (a client, a place's history, a person's habit) may be invented; a shared fact that other content relies on needs the designer's approval. For a question of voice, give two competing drafts.
